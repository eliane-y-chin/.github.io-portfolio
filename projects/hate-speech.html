Distinguishing Hate Speech from Offensive Language and Analysing Model Errors

Content note: this project deals with hateful and offensive language. Slurs are described by type and never quoted on this page.

Content moderation needs to separate speech that attacks people for who they are from speech that is merely rude. The two share most of their vocabulary. This project compares four classifiers on 24,783 tweets, selects the one that misses the least hate speech, and traces the patterns behind its mistakes.

Why accuracy is the wrong score here

The project uses the Davidson et al. (2017) dataset: tweets labelled by crowd workers as hate speech (5.8%), offensive language (77.4%) or neither, i.e. neutral (16.8%). A model that labels every tweet "offensive" scores 77% accuracy and catches no hate speech at all.

Models were therefore judged on macro-F1, which weights all three classes equally, and on hate speech recall: the share of real hate speech the model catches. To help the model learn the rare class, I used stratified 80/10/10 splits and weighted the training loss so that hate speech counted about 13 times more than offensive language.

Exploratory analysis showed the core difficulty: almost every frequent word in the hate class also appears, often more frequently, in the offensive class. Tweet length was nearly identical across classes. Whatever separates the two lies in context, not in vocabulary.

Four models, one decision

I compared TF-IDF logistic regression with fine-tuned BERT, each in two designs: a flat model that predicts all three classes at once, and a two-stage model that first separates toxic from neutral tweets, then hate from offensive.

Why not the model with the best score? Two-stage BERT had the highest macro-F1, but it caught 11 percentage points less hate speech. In moderation, a missed hateful post that stays online does more harm than an offensive post flagged for review, so I chose flat BERT for its recall.

BERT beat logistic regression on every metric: its contextual representations pick up differences that word counts cannot. The two-stage design did not help either model, and the next section shows why.

The two-stage design loses data at the first gate

Where the chosen model goes wrong

Flat BERT made 214 hate/offensive mistakes on the test set: 181 offensive tweets flagged as hate and 33 hate tweets missed. The high class weight makes it deliberately sensitive. To find patterns, I embedded the misclassified tweets with Sentence-BERT and grouped them with K-means; the elbow method suggested six clusters.

2 clusters · 82 tweets

Racial slurs in casual, in-group conversation. Reclaimed language between friends is hard to tell from a targeted attack in a short tweet.

2 clusters · 58 tweets

Homophobic slurs used as generic insults, not aimed at LGBTQ+ people. The words are strong hate signals, but the model applies them without context.

1 cluster · 28 tweets

Ableist slurs used casually; the same over-reliance on a single word.

1 cluster · 46 tweets

Mixed insults with no clear target. The only cluster with errors in both directions: the model is genuinely unsure.

The common thread: the model doesn't miss slurs; it misreads context. It cannot tell who is being targeted, by whom, or how, from one short tweet. This also matches published evidence that this dataset over-labels African American English as hateful, so a strong score here is not proof of fairness.

What I'd tell a moderation team

  1. Optimise for recall on the harmful class, and choose the metric before choosing the model.
  2. Route uncertain cases to humans. When the model was unsure whether a tweet was hate or offensive (predicted probability between 0.4 and 0.6), it was right on only 52 of 98 test tweets (53%), barely better than a coin flip. These are the posts a human reviewer should check.
  3. Add context, not complexity. A second stage didn't help; conversation history or information about the target would.
  4. Audit for dialect bias before deployment, using fairness metrics broken down by dialect.

My role

I led the methodology, modelling and evaluation. Parts of the code were generated with Claude Code, then manually checked and revised by me.

Limitations: the results come from a single dataset of tweets, so they may not carry over to other platforms; only one way of weighting the classes was tested; and the project does not show which words drove each BERT prediction.
