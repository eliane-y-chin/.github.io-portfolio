Finding the Target Customers for a Subscription Offer

Using course case data for an online retailer, I compared six ways of choosing which customers should receive a mailed subscription offer, and found the approach that turned a loss-making campaign into a profitable one.

The problem: mailing everyone loses money

The retailer wanted to mail a discounted subscription offer to its customers. Each letter has a cost, and each customer who subscribes brings in a profit. Dividing one by the other gives a break-even response rate: any group of customers less likely than that to respond costs more to mail than it earns.

Too few customers subscribed overall, so mailing everyone lost money. The task was to find the customers worth mailing.

What I compared

RFM segments

Grouped customers by recency, frequency and spend, then mailed only the groups whose response rate beat break-even. This made the campaign profitable.

K-means

Clustered customers on the standardised RFM variables (elbow method). Profitable, but the weakest approach, because clustering ignores who actually responds.

Decision tree

Learned directly which customers subscribe, with recency as the first split. It gave the highest return of all approaches and is the easiest to explain to a marketing team.

Random forest

Strong with default settings; limiting tree depth reduced overfitting and improved it further, but it still fell slightly short of the single decision tree.

Takeaway: models trained on the actual outcome (did the customer subscribe?) beat segmentations that only describe customers. The simplest supervised model won here, and it is also the easiest one to hand to a campaign manager.

What I'd recommend

  1. Set a break-even response rate before every campaign, and only mail segments predicted to beat it.
  2. Use a supervised model such as a decision tree for targeting, and validate it on customers it hasn't seen.

Other methods from this module

  • Designing A/B/N tests, including power calculations to size the test

About this project

MSc coursework for UCL's Marketing Analytics module, written in R with dplyr, rpart and ranger. The business scenario and data were provided by the course and do not come from a real company. Detailed figures are omitted because the case is still used in teaching.
